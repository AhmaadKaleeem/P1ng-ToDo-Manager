# Copywriting

Copywriting is not the same job as creative writing or technical documentation. It is writing built to move one specific reader toward one specific action. Every decision below, which framework, how technical, which words to cut, serves that one goal.

## Step 1. Name the task before writing anything

Ask what format this is and what single action the reader should take after reading it. The format decides almost everything else, so do not skip this step even when the request seems obvious.

Common tasks this skill covers.

- Headline or hook (ad, article title, hero line)
- Short ad copy (search, social, display)
- Landing page hero and full page
- Single email
- Email sequence or nurture series
- Product description
- Long-form sales page
- CTA button or microcopy
- Case study or testimonial framing
- Technical product copy (spec sheets, feature pages, API or developer-facing pages)

## Step 2. Pick a framework that matches the task

These frameworks come from established direct-response copywriting practice, not from one source. Each one is a scaffold, not a script. Fill it with real specifics about the actual product and reader.

**AIDA**, attention, interest, desire, action. A reader moves from noticing the message to wanting the outcome to acting on it. Good for landing pages and ads where you have room to build interest gradually.

**PAS**, problem, agitation, solution. Name the problem, make the reader feel its weight, then present the fix. Good for short ads and emails aimed at a reader who already has a clear pain point.

**BAB**, before, after, bridge. Show life before the product, life after, then the product as the bridge between the two. Good for case studies, testimonials, and transformation stories.

**FAB**, feature, advantage, benefit. State what the thing has, what that lets it do, and what that means for the reader. Good for product descriptions and spec-heavy pages where features need translating into outcomes.

**4Ps**, promise, picture, proof, push. Make a promise, help the reader picture it delivered, back it with proof, then ask for the action. Good for sales pages with strong social proof to lean on.

**PASTOR**, problem, amplify, story, transformation, offer, response. A longer version of PAS built for full sales pages that need a narrative arc.

**QUEST**, qualify, understand, educate, stimulate, transition. Built for email sequences, since it spreads persuasion across several messages instead of one.

### Task to framework quick reference

| Task | Framework to reach for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Headline or hook | Four Us (useful, urgent, unique, ultra-specific) | Headlines live or die on specificity, not structure |
| Short ad (search, social) | PAS or AIDA | Compact and hook-driven, little room for a slow build |
| Landing page hero | AIDA or 4Ps | Needs a promise the reader can picture fast |
| Full landing page | AIDA, with FAB in the features section | Combine frameworks by section rather than forcing one to do everything |
| Single email | PAS | One problem, one push |
| Email sequence | QUEST | Spreads the persuasion job across multiple sends |
| Product description | FAB | Every feature needs an explicit "which means" |
| Long-form sales page | PASTOR or AIDA | Needs sustained narrative, not just a hook |
| Case study | BAB | The whole point is showing a transformation |
| CTA button or microcopy | Action plus outcome, not a framework | "See your savings," not "Submit" |

## Step 3. Decide how technical to write

This decision matters more than the framework choice, and it is the one most drafts get wrong. Technical copywriting and non-technical copywriting are not different levels of quality, they are different tools for different readers.

**Write technical copy when** the reader evaluates the purchase with expertise of their own, an engineer, an IT buyer, a procurement team, a doctor reading about a device. They are not spending their own money casually, they often have to justify the choice to someone else, and vague language reads as a red flag rather than a relief. Lead with the mechanism, be exact about numbers and specs, and treat plain language as a tool for clarity, not a way to avoid sounding smart. A benefit still belongs on the page, but it follows the fact instead of replacing it.

**Write non-technical copy when** the reader is deciding for themselves, based on how the outcome will make their life better, easier, or more enjoyable. Specs and mechanisms matter far less than the reader's day-to-day, their frustration, or the feeling they want. Simple words, shorter sentences, and a benefit stated up front all do more work here than precision does.

**Most B2B technical products need both, in the same piece.** A SaaS buyer or a hardware engineer still wants proof the thing works, not just a feeling, but they also do not want a spec sheet with no reason attached. The fix is to state the technical fact, then translate it in the same sentence or the one right after it.

Example translation.
Fact alone, weak. "The API supports batched requests up to 500 per call."
Feeling alone, weak. "Move faster than ever with our powerful platform."
Translated, strong. "The API batches up to 500 requests per call, so a sync job that used to take twenty minutes now takes about ninety seconds."

### Side by side, same product, two readers

Product, a home battery backup system.

Technical audience (installer, engineer specifying the system).
"10 kWh LFP battery, 5 kW continuous output, 7.6 kW peak, round-trip efficiency 95 percent, UL 9540 certified, compatible with split-phase 120/240V panels without a sub-panel."

Non-technical audience (homeowner).
"Enough stored power to run your fridge, lights, and Wi-Fi through a multi-day outage, and it kicks in before you even notice the power went out."

Same underlying facts. Different reader, different job for the sentence.

## Step 4. Draft, then check it against real signs of AI-sounding writing

The signs below come from patterns documented by Wikipedia's WikiProject AI Cleanup, an editor group that catalogs recurring traits in AI-generated text. Their own guide is explicit that these are observations, useful in combination, not absolute rules that prove a passage was AI-written. Use this list as a diagnostic pass after drafting, not as a checklist to satisfy while writing, since writing defensively against a checklist tends to produce its own kind of stiff, hedged prose.

Watch for these while reviewing a draft.

- **Vague attribution.** "Experts say" or "studies show" with no one named. Name the source or cut the claim.
- **Weak "-ing" endings.** A sentence that closes by tacking on a significance claim, "helping cement its place as a leading choice." Cut it or replace it with a concrete fact.
- **Rule of three.** Reflexively grouping everything in triplets, "fast, reliable, and affordable." One strong claim beats three generic ones.
- **Negative parallelism.** "It's not a product, it's a movement." Fine once in a long piece, tiresome as a habit.
- **The "from X to Y" filler.** "Solutions ranging from strategic planning to execution" that names no actual range. State what you offer instead.
- **Restating the point.** Summarizing what the reader just read as though they will forget it in three sentences. Trust them and move on.
- **Overstating importance.** "A critical moment" or "a key milestone" attached to something ordinary. Let the fact carry its own weight.
- **Promotional puffery with no specifics behind it.** "Industry-leading," "world-class," "best-in-class" without a number or a name to back it up.
- **Template symmetry.** Every paragraph the same length, every list the same structure. Vary the rhythm the way a person naturally would.

## Step 5. House style for this skill's own output

These next two rules are a stylistic choice for this skill's output, not claims about universal signs of AI writing. Apply them because they were requested, not because avoiding them alone makes writing sound human.

- Do not use em dashes.
- Do not use colons.
- Avoid AI-associated filler vocabulary, "leverage," "utilize," "delve," "robust," "seamless," "unlock," "elevate," "game-changing," "cutting-edge," "in today's world," "additionally," "furthermore." Say the plain version instead.

## Step 6. Before delivering, check the draft against this list

- Does the reader know the one action to take next, and is it stated plainly.
- Does the register match the reader, technical facts for a technical reader, feeling and outcome for a non-technical one, or both where the audience needs both.
- Is every claimed benefit attached to a real feature, proof point, or number.
- Read it out loud. If a sentence sounds like it belongs in a press release rather than something a person would say, rewrite it.
- Scan for the AI-sounding patterns in Step 4 one more time, now that the draft is finished rather than while it was being written.
